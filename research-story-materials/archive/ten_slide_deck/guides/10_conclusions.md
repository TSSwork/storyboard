## What to say
Let me close by answering the question I started with.

Can observed rainfall improve one-month-ahead forecasts of vegetable prices? For cabbage, brinjal and carrot, yes, beyond what past prices alone provide, and the result survived correction for multiple testing and a range of alternative choices. For snake gourd, pumpkin and beans, no reliable improvement; tomato is a maybe I treat as exploratory. The value of rainfall is specific to each commodity, and that is the main finding.

I also showed three other dimensions of the food system, paddy, rice imports and exchange rates, as separate pieces of context.

Now the honest limits, because they matter as much. I looked at this test period during a pilot, so these are exploratory results, not an independent confirmation. The rainfall measures are proxies, not rainfall in the actual growing areas, and 62 forecast errors are related to each other. The wider analyses are short series with crisis years in them, incomplete trade data and no inflation deflator yet.

And what this is not: it is not a forecasting product. The forecasts are far from precise. The contribution is knowing where rainfall adds information and where it does not.

What would I do next? Test on newly published months, find rainfall for the real growing areas, and add the deflator so the exchange-rate question can be answered properly. Thank you.

## Key points to land
- Rainfall adds out-of-sample information for cabbage, brinjal and carrot; the value is commodity-specific.
- The results are exploratory: the test window was seen in the pilot, and the rainfall series are proxies.
- This is a research finding, not a forecasting tool; next steps are new months, growing-area rainfall and a deflator.

## In plain words
Rain helps forecast some vegetable prices and not others. It is not precise, it needs confirming on new data, and it is one piece of a bigger food-system picture.

## For the technical evaluator
Next steps in order of value: (1) out-of-sample evaluation on months published after the study, which would give a clean test; (2) rainfall for the true growing areas, replacing national and box proxies; (3) a CPI or NCPI deflator to move the exchange-rate analysis from co-movement to a pass-through estimate. Longer horizons would need rainfall forecasts and are a separate study.

## If someone asks
**"What is the single most important finding?"**  
That the usefulness of rainfall information is commodity-specific. It is not a universal property of vegetable prices.

**"How confident are you in the cabbage and brinjal results?"**  
Reasonably, for this period: they pass a corrected test and several robustness checks. But I looked at the period before, so I would want to see them hold on new months before relying on them.

**"What would change your conclusion?"**  
If the gains disappeared on months published after this study, or if growing-area rainfall changed the ranking, I would revise it.

**"Could a business use this?"**  
Not as it stands. It shows where rainfall carries information, which could guide a future forecasting project, but the forecasts are not accurate enough to act on.
